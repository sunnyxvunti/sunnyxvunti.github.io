Mercer Undergraduate Research Symposium / MURS 2026

Evaluating AI output quality through the discipline of DMAIC.

Instead of assuming that a better prompt always produces a better answer, I designed a structured experiment to examine how models and prompting strategies performed across process-improvement tasks.

The research question

How do model selection and prompt strategy affect the usefulness of generated content for Lean Six Sigma problem-solving? The research compares outputs against a defined evaluation rubric, rather than relying only on anecdotes.

Study design

I designed and evaluated a 450-output experiment comparing Claude, GPT-4, and Gemini across Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, and Control tasks and three prompting approaches: zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought. Outputs were judged against a five-dimension rubric.

Methods and findings

Using Minitab and GLM ANOVA, the study found that model selection was the dominant observed performance factor (F = 35.21, p < 0.001) within the study design; prompt variant also showed a secondary effect (F = 4.47, p = 0.012). I presented the work through Mercer's undergraduate research program in 2026.

Practical deliverables

I also developed an AI-assisted value-stream-mapping framework to encode standard process elements—material and information flows, inventory triangles, process data boxes, and lead-time representation—toward structured current/future-state maps.

What this does not prove

The results depend on this task set and rubric. They do not establish that one model is best for every business workflow or that generated content is safe to deploy without human review.
