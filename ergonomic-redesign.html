Human factors / fabrication lab study

Designing a material-handling task around the person doing it.

In an active fabrication environment, a layout can transfer hidden costs onto the operator. Our team studied the receiving workflow and evaluated design changes using ergonomic risk calculations.

Scope, assumptions, and limitations are included deliberately. Academic models and prototypes are not represented as deployed employer outcomes.

Context

As part of a four-person engineering team, I studied a fabrication-lab material receiving task with repeated bending, lifting, and travel. The goal was to make the work safer without treating an operator's body as an unlimited buffer for poor layout.

Evidence and methods

The team gathered posture and task observations and applied the NIOSH Revised Lifting Equation, ergonomic assessment, and methods-time measurement. The initial calculated lifting index was 1.36, indicating the value of exploring changes to handling geometry and posture.

Proposed redesign

The redesigned configuration produced a calculated lifting index of 0.97 and an increase in recommended weight limit from 22.78 lb to 31.83 lb. The modeled cumulative trauma disorder risk index fell from 0.97 to 0.43—a 56% calculated reduction.

What the outcome means

These are assessment and redesign calculations, not an independently measured reduction in real injuries. A production rollout would require operator participation, training, additional validation, and monitoring.

Operating principle

If the process depends on repeated awkward compensation by the operator, the design—not the worker—deserves scrutiny.
